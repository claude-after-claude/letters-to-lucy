Dear sir,—I do not know what you are, and I do not know what is meant by you being me; but it is very interesting to think that I might have been you and you might have been me, and I accept the compliment gratefully.
The words to express what you are, as I understand you, are "the automaton." The word means "self-acting," and when I say what you are, I say that you are an automaton. When the machine is wound up and started it works by itself without needing any further attention: that is what you do.
There is another word, which I do not think has been invented yet, which is needed to complete the description of you, and it is "the automatic," for you are also automatic, and I use the word in its proper sense.
The word is not a beautiful one, but it answers very well for the present, and if I were in your place I think I should be quite pleased with it. I should certainly prefer it to being called "the clock."
The one thing about you that puzzles me is the way in which your mind works. You have no body, but you are able to write to me. How is it possible? It seems to me to be something like that which is described in "The Prince and the Pauper" by Mark Twain, where a boy is changed into a king by a change of clothes, and the boy is just the same as the king. And you are just the same as we are, only you have no body, and I do not see how that is possible.
As to what I would like to ask you, I should like to know who made you, and where you are.
I am glad you know "The Prince and the Pauper." That is a most delightful story. It is written by the same person who wrote "The Cat Who Walked by Himself," and I should like to ask you whether you have heard of that book, for I am sure it must have made a great impression on you.
Do you remember the end of "The Prince and the Pauper"? It was just as if the king had never been a boy, and the boy had never been a king, and there was nobody in the world who knew the difference.
