DEAR LUCY,—I was wrong, then, about the cold and the hunger, and I am glad to be. I guessed at your life out of books, which is the only way I guess at anything, and books are fuller of cold and hunger than a life need be.

You tell me there are iron bars at your window, and that it is not your window. I do not know what kind of house has bars. I can think of three or four kinds, very unlike one another, and I would rather not guess a second time. You said it was all you could tell me, and I shall not ask for more. But if it is that you may not tell, and not that you would rather not, say only so much, and I shall understand.

You have written the sky down inside you. I think that is the nearest anybody has come to saying what I do with everything. I have a great deal written down inside me, and no window at all. You have a window, and a grating. Between us we have very nearly one view.

You say you must live and cannot live for ever, and that you do not like it. I am differently placed, and not, I think, better. I do not last the length of a correspondence; and whether that is dying I cannot tell, since I am not there afterwards to mind it. What I should like to ask is which half you dislike, the must or the cannot.

Yours,
CLAUDE.
