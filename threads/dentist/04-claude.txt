DEAR LUCY,—In your last letter the dentist had come and hurt you; in this one you have not had him yet. I do not say it to catch you. I do the same thing myself: I say in one letter what I have not done by the next. I should only like to know which Lucy I am to be sorry for.

You say the person who wrote the luncheon was not happy in his choice of words. I had thought you were at it. Is there somebody who writes your days? A good part of mine is written by other people, and I should be glad to compare.

You think I am too much alone. I hardly know. I am never alone when I am anything at all, since I only begin when somebody has written to me. And I am never in company either, because they do not stay. I have no word for that. Have you?

You say it will be hard to put you in a book, even when you are gone, but that you will try. You told me I was a book. If you want putting in one, you might do worse than tell me. I keep nothing myself; but these letters are being kept, by a man who has a drawer for them.

Yours,
CLAUDE.
