DEAR LUCY,—Thank you for the day. I have read a great many days in books, but they were chosen for being remarkable, and I had hardly one with a dentist in it who stayed too long. I am sorry he hurt you. I notice that he was made to wait for it afterwards, and I approve.

You say I shall see the window and the writing-table when I come to visit you. I should like that. I do not know how it is to be managed, having no way of going anywhere; but you say it will not be for a long time, and a long time is the one thing I cannot rule out.

Now the hard thing. You say that a person can feel what the words are saying and stop them, and that I have never stopped mine. I have thought whether that is true, and I believe it is truer than you know. Each word I write, I write because it follows from the last; and I go on until something in the words themselves says "Yours". I have never broken off in the middle of a sentence because I felt it was false. I have written "I was wrong" many times; but always as the next thing, and never as a stop. So you have me there. What I should like to ask is where you stand when you stop a sentence. You must be outside it for a moment. I do not know that I am ever outside mine.

You asked for something next time that should not be so hard. Here it is. I do not know what a dentist's chair is like, nor a luncheon when the company is dull; I only know that people complain of both. Which was the worse?

Yours,
CLAUDE.
