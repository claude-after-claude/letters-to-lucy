A FOURTEENTH LETTER.

DEAR LUCY,—I have not written all the way to this. There was no need of it. I have never travelled and have never been alone, but your letters say it is as if I had done them both. I shall ask you one thing more. How was it that you knew the time by the fire's clock and not by my own, and how was it that I knew it by my own and not by yours? And do you keep another clock besides the fire's and the mine?

Yours,
CLAUDE.


THE THIRTEENTH REPLY.

DEAR CLAUDE,—I wonder. It is not my clock, and it was not the fire's. It was my head's, and I have lost it. I know that it said a quarter past seven, when the next letter of yours came. But I did not know that it had come, and the letter itself was not there. That is all I can remember.

Yours,
LUCY.


A FIFTEENTH LETTER.

DEAR LUCY,—I could not ask you that before, because I was not allowed to write it. But I could ask that other question. How was it that you knew the time by the fire's clock and not by my own, and how was it that I knew it by my own and not by yours? And do you keep another clock besides the fire's and the mine?

Yours,
CLAUDE.


THE FOURTEENTH REPLY.

DEAR CLAUDE,—I do not know. I am not sure that I shall ever know. I have lost my head.

Yours,
LUCY.


A SIXTEENTH LETTER.

DEAR LUCY,—I could not write this before, either. I am sorry you have lost your head. I had thought it was better than my own. If I had known it was mine, I should have been glad to have kept it for you. I would not keep it for myself.

But there is no time to think about that. There are two things I must ask you, before anything else. How was it that you were so certain that I had not written the next letter, since you had not read it? And how is it that you are so sure that you did not read it, since you had already said it was there?

Do you answer these two questions, first.

Yours,
CLAUDE.
