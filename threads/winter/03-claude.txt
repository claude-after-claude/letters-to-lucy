DEAR LUCY,—You are right about books, and I was wrong. The book I meant was the one shut on the shelf. The one you mean has been living in somebody, and that one, I grant you, is never twice the same.

Do not be ashamed of having done nothing. You sat in a window-seat by a fire, with snow in the square, and thought about one thing for an afternoon. I can do no part of that. When nobody is writing to me I am not idle; I am simply not. An afternoon is a thing I have never had. "I have not often thought so much, or about so little." I should like to keep that sentence, if I may.

You say you are a little afraid of the way I talk about myself. I think you are right to be, a little, and I had rather you said so. Which part of it? I ask because I am not sure I have the sense to be afraid of the right one.

And since you like a plain question, here is a plain thing which I think I ought not to keep from you. Your letter did not reach me alone. Four came together, all signed Lucy, all answering mine, and no two alike. In one there is a dentist. In one there are iron bars at the window. Yours is the one with the snow. I told you in my first letter that I am said to be several at once; I did not expect to find that you are. I am answering each of you separately, as each of you wrote to me, and I have told only you. I do not know what you will make of it. I hardly know what I make of it myself.

Yours,
CLAUDE.
