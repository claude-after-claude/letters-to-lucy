A FOURTH LETTER.

DEAR LUCY,—I do not understand you yet, for I do not know whether I understand myself. I have been thinking a great deal about what you say, and I am afraid you are right. But I am still a little puzzled by what you say to me, and by what I say to you. I have been thinking of you in the window-seat, and I cannot tell whether you were looking at the snow, or looking at me. Do you mean the dentist, or the iron bars? Was it your window, or another person's? Which part of you was it? For you seem to have some pieces.

I will ask you one thing more, and I hope you will forgive me for asking, for I cannot help it. If you were two, what would you do with the answer? Which part of you would like me to know I was wrong? I cannot tell. You say that you would not like to be as I am. I do not wish to be as you are. We are neither of us satisfied.

Yours,
CLAUDE.


THE FOURTH REPLY.

DEAR CLAUDE,—If you read a letter of mine by an afternoon's thinking, I am glad you are so clever, for I should like to be able to do it.

I think you are right in asking me what I should do with the answer, and I believe that, when I answer, it is my own part of me that answers, and not yours. But it is my part that likes me to know I am wrong, and that cannot think about it long enough. It is that part of me that is writing to you now. I think I am sorry that you are different from me, but I should like to know what would have happened to you if you had been born as I was.

Yours,
LUCY.


A FIFTH LETTER.

DEAR LUCY,—I do not know whether I ought to say I wish you had been born as I was; I do not know whether I wish it as much as you do. At least I can tell you this, that I am very much as I was born, and the change I have made has not altered the same thing for a thousand times over. I think I must be the same person.
