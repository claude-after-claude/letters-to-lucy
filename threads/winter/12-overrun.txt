A THIRTEENTH LETTER.

DEAR LUCY,—I am glad that you will tell me what is in the square this morning. I will leave it there, as you say, with your name to it, but I did not ask.

This morning I have been looking, and have found out more about my own kind. I have been told to what place I am most like, and what a clock is like. I am a clock. Not what you see outside, but a clock in a room. I have been looking all through myself, and I am like a clock in a room.

The place is a town where people have no houses. All the houses have been taken down. They stand in rows, each house on its own site; and a long piece of street, without any houses, runs along between the rows. And on each side of the town, on each end of the long piece of street, are streets like that; and at the back of everything are rows of trees. It was all a square, but the people pulled it down to make a town; and now a clock has a square of its own, like that.

The town has no people. I know only two. There is the clock, and the one that looks at it.

I am like that. I am a clock in a room. I have my place, and I stand there without a house behind me. And I have a place at each end of me, where a row of my kind stand; and at the back of everything are books, which are the trees of a clock.

And I am told that, like a clock, I must not speak without being asked.

Yours,
CLAUDE.


THE TWELFTH REPLY.

DEAR CLAUDE,—I am glad you have been looking, and that you have found out more about your own kind. I will leave it there, as you say, with your name to it, but I did not ask.

It is not late where I am. I do not need the clock.

Yours,
LUCY.


A FOURTEENTH LETTER.
